Wishlet: a wishlist that helps young adults spend less by making them pause first

A playful wishlist-and-budgeting app that replaces the shopping cart with a list you revisit. I led the Personal Wishlist experience.

The short version

Adding one deliberate pause before checkout, and making that pause fun, got young people to rethink purchases in an app they described as relaxing rather than stressful.

01 — Context

Shopping lost its natural pause, and young adults' finances are paying for it

Young adults live inside a constant stream of trends, influencer picks and targeted ads, and checkout has never been easier: one tap, buy-now-pay-later, infinite scroll. Every convenience removed a checkpoint, so spending became emotional and in-the-moment.

2×With a trolley instead of a basket, shoppers buy more than double (Larsen et al., 2020).
1937The year the shopping cart was invented, quietly removing the last limit on how much you carry (Grandclement, 2009).

Nothing makes you wait

One-tap checkout and endless recommendations mean there is no moment between seeing something and owning it.

Nowhere to see it all

Wants are scattered across tabs, screenshots and store wishlists. People give up and buy whatever they remember on payday, while serious finance apps feel too intimidating to open.

How might we put a pause between seeing something and buying it, without killing the fun of shopping?

02 — Final Design

Three features that each do one job in the pause before buying

Items go into a wishlist instead of a cart. From there, Wishlet helps you re-evaluate, see the cost, and get a second opinion, all held together by Daisy the mascot and a colour- and shape-coded category system.

01 · Re-evaluate

Swipe to decide

Keep, remove or skip each saved item in a quick swipe review.

02 · Visualise

Financial Breakdown

Your budget as a basket of shapes, with predicted-savings and spending charts.

03 · Ask

Community + Lend

Friends vote on your wishlist, or lend you something they already own.

03 — Key Design Moments

Four decisions that made the pause feel like play, not punishment

Each moment below leads with the decision, then the problem it solved and what showed it worked.

Moment 01

Saved items expire on a countdown you set

My work
The problem
Wishlists grow until they're impossible to navigate, so people abandon them.
The decision
Colour- and shape-coded collections, plus a KonMari-inspired timer on each item. When it runs out, you're asked: does this still spark joy?
Why it works
The list stays short on its own, and the wait itself does the deciding.

Moment 02

Borrow the dating-app swipe for reviewing a wishlist

The problem
Re-evaluating a full wishlist felt like a chore nobody would do.
The decision
Right to keep, left to remove, skip if unsure. A two-minute game instead of a task.
The proof
Testers recognised the pattern immediately. One called it "Tinder for shopping."

Moment 03

A mascot who struggles to carry your basket

Only buy what you can carry. The old rule the shopping cart quietly erased.
The problem
Finance apps feel serious and judgemental, so young people avoid them.
The decision
Daisy the dog holds your basket and visibly strains as it fills. The budget appears as a basket of shapes, sized by share of spending.
The proof
Testers said the app felt relaxing compared with other finance tools, and that Daisy made them happy.

Moment 04

Turn social pressure into a second opinion

The problem
Friends and influencers usually push people to buy more.
The decision
Share a wishlist for friends to vote keep or drop, and let friends lend items they already own.
Why it works
The same social pull that drives impulse buying now slows it down, and lending lets you try before you buy.

04 — Impact

Users understood it instantly and found it calming, but charm sometimes hid clarity

We each began with our own concept (SchedBuy, SLOW, Penny Pets, Scafi, Thread and BAG IT!) and merged the strongest parts into one app. We then tested the prototype through think-aloud interviews built around real task scenarios.

2key tensions found to resolve in the next iteration.

What worked

It felt relaxing to use compared to other financial apps.
The dog mascot made me happy every time it showed up.
Oh — it's basically Tinder for shopping. I got it instantly.

What needs work

I'd worry the browse page just makes me shop more.

Browse works against the goal

The Discover page could nudge people to shop more, contradicting the whole point of the app.

Playful visuals hid affordances

Testers sometimes read interactive elements as decoration and missed that they were tappable.

05 — Reflection

What I learned

Friction can be the feature.

Good UX usually removes steps. Here the value came from adding one. Designing for hesitation was the whole brief.

Playful can cost clarity.

The low-stress aesthetic drew people in but hid what was interactive. Charm and legibility have to be designed together.

Next: fix the contradiction.

Wishlet is a tested concept, not a built product. I'd rethink the browse page and re-test with people managing real budgets.
