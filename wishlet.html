Building Wishlet, to put a pause between wanting and buying

Wishlet is a wishlist-and-budgeting app my team designed for young adults who impulse-shop online. Instead of another spreadsheet-serious finance tool, we made saving playful and social — turning the moment before checkout into a chance to pause, see the cost, and ask a friend. I led the Personal Wishlist experience.

Role
UX Research · UI Design
Tools

01 — Context

A generation that shops faster than it can think

Young adults live inside a constant stream of trends, influencer picks and targeted ads — and checkout has never been easier. One tap, buy-now-pay-later, infinite scroll. Spending becomes emotional and in-the-moment, disconnected from what someone actually needs or can afford. Our problem domain was blunt about it: the younger generation is slipping into impulse-buying habits that set up poor finances for later.

02 — The Problem

When buying is frictionless, so is regret

Every convenience that made shopping easier also removed a natural checkpoint. Wants pile up across tabs, screenshots and store wishlists until they are impossible to navigate — so people give up, close everything, and on payday buy the first thing they remember. Then they regret not saving it for what they actually wanted.

Nothing makes you wait

One-tap checkout and endless recommendations mean there is no moment between seeing something and owning it. The impulse and the purchase have collapsed into a single gesture.

Nowhere to see it all

Desired items are scattered across apps with no single view of what you want or what it would cost. And traditional finance apps feel so serious and intimidating that young people avoid them entirely.

How might we put a pause between seeing something and buying it — without killing the fun of shopping?

03 — A Proposed Solution

Save it, sit with it, decide together

Wishlet catches the purchase before it happens: instead of a cart, items go into a wishlist you actually revisit. The concept rests on three moves — re-evaluate what you saved, see what it costs, and get a second opinion — all wrapped in a warm, playful interface built to break the "finance app = stressful" stereotype.

Wishlet wish·let — verb. To save a thing, and sit with it a while before you buy.

Daisy, a dog mascot, holds your basket in the corner of the app and visibly struggles as your wishlist fills — a gentle, non-judgemental nudge to review before you spend, borrowed from the old rule of "only buy what you can carry."

Save it now. Decide later.

04 — Features

Three features, one habit

Each feature does one job in the pause between wanting and buying — re-evaluate, visualise, and get a second opinion — tied together by Daisy and a colour- and shape-coded system so categories are recognisable at a glance.

01

Swipe to decide

The Wishlist Review borrows the dating-app swipe: right to keep, left to remove, skip if you're unsure. Re-evaluating a full wishlist becomes a two-minute game instead of a chore.

02

Financial Breakdown

Your budget shown as a basket of coloured shapes, plus predicted-savings and spending charts — so you see where the money is going at a glance, without a spreadsheet.

03

Community + Lend

Share a wishlist with friends who vote to keep or drop each item. If a friend already owns something, they can "lend" it — so you try before you buy.

05 — Findings

From six ideas to one people wanted

We each started with our own concept — SchedBuy, SLOW, Penny Pets, Scafi, Thread and BAG IT! — then funnelled the strongest parts into one app: the swipe review from the dating-app idea, cost visualisation from Scafi and SLOW, and the community layer from BAG IT! and Thread. We tested the prototype with a think-aloud protocol across twelve interviews.

What we heard

It felt relaxing to use compared to other financial apps.
The dog mascot made me happy every time it showed up.
Oh — it's basically Tinder for shopping. I got it instantly.

Two honest tensions surfaced. The Browse / Discover page could nudge people to shop more — working against the whole point of the app. And the playful visuals sometimes read as decoration, so testers missed that elements were tappable. Charm was getting in the way of clarity.

06 — Reflection

What I took away

My focus was the Personal Wishlist — collections that are colour- and shape-coded, and items that quietly expire after a countdown you set, a KonMari-inspired "does this still spark joy?" nudge. Designing it put me right at the tension at the heart of the whole app: making finance feel unintimidating without making it unreadable.

Playful can cost clarity

The cute, low-stress aesthetic got people in the door — but testers mistook interactive elements for decoration. Charm and legibility have to be designed together, not traded off.

Friction can be the feature

Everywhere else, good UX removes steps. Here the entire value was adding one — the pause before buying. Designing for hesitation was the whole brief.

Still a concept

High-fidelity and user-tested, but not built. The real next step is resolving the browse-page contradiction and re-testing with people managing real budgets.
